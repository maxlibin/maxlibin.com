I shipped app #17 this week.

That's 17 apps in 2026 — 17 different products, each solving some specific problem, each built with AI tools, each deployed to production. Sixteen of them are live right now. Some have paying users. Most don't.

Here's the number nobody talks about on Twitter when they post their latest vibe-coded launch: **out of my 16 shipped apps, only 4 have crossed $100 in revenue.** Two are doing well enough to cover their infrastructure costs. The other fourteen? They're digital tumbleweeds with beautiful landing pages.

The "Vibe Code to Glory" challenge was supposed to be about proving that one person with AI can ship 24 revenue-ready apps in 12 months. The *shipping* part turned out to be easy. The *revenue-ready* part? That's been the real education.

## The AI Paradox Nobody Mentions

AI tools made building almost frictionless. I can go from idea to deployed app in a weekend — sometimes less. GetFileMock.com took 4 hours. Tulsk's core took a week. MyPhotoAI was roughly 3 days of prompt-and-iterate.

But here's the paradox: **when building costs near-zero, distribution becomes the only thing that matters.** And distribution is still hard.

The industry numbers confirm this. In 2026, 63% of people using AI coding tools are non-developers — product managers, founders, designers. The barrier to entry isn't technical skill anymore. It's having an idea and the willingness to describe it to an agent. The result? The App Store saw 557,000 new apps last year, a 24% surge driven by AI-assisted development. Every niche I think of, someone else has already shipped into.

Code is not the moat. Distribution is.

## What I Got Wrong the First 8 Times

My first 8 apps — GetFileMock, Seekdome, Tryout, aitinerary.ai, and a few others — share a pattern. I built them, put a "Show HN" out, posted on Twitter/X, and waited.

Most got between 0 and 12 signups. Total.

Here's what I was doing wrong:

**1. I built for problems I'd already solved.** GetFileMock generates dummy test files. I needed that tool myself, so I built it. It's useful. But developers who need a 50MB test file have already found their own workflow — curl a random URL, write a dd command, or scavenge from their downloads folder. My tool was marginally better, but not enough to make anyone switch habits.

**2. I optimized for building, not for finding.** I spent 40 hours building and 2 hours on distribution. That ratio needs to invert. The solo founders who win in 2026 are the ones who spend 20% of their time building and 80% on distribution.

**3. I priced like a big company.** For Tulsk, I set up a pricing page with Free, $39/mo, and $99/mo tiers — modeled after what a SaaS company with a sales team would charge. I had zero users and a pricing page designed for a Series A startup. I was solving the wrong problem.

## The Two Apps That Actually Work

Let me tell you about the two apps that crossed the threshold into real usage.

**Interior AI** (iOS) — This one took off because I didn't try to sell it. I posted a 30-second video on X showing me pointing my phone at my cluttered desk, selecting "minimal zen," and watching it transform. The video got 14,000 views. Not because I'm a good marketer (I'm not), but because the demo *showed* the value before anyone had to pay for it.

The lesson: **demonstration beats explanation.** Every time. People need to see the thing work before they'll give you their email, let alone their credit card.

**HomeworkAI** — This one worked because I went where the users already were. Instead of building a landing page and running ads, I joined three Singapore parent Telegram groups (30,000+ members total) and started answering questions about PSLE math at 10 PM. When parents saw my answers, they asked "what tool are you using?" I told them about HomeworkAI. That was my distribution channel.

## What I'm Doing Differently for Apps #17–24

Based on what's worked and what hasn't, here's the playbook I'm running for the remaining 8 apps:

### 1. Ship First, Name the Price Later

For apps #9–16, I started with a simple approach: make it work, put a single low price, and iterate. Zhouyi Master AI launched at $3.99 one-time. No subscription. No free tier. Just "here's the app, here's what it costs."

You know what I learned from the pricing debates I used to have in my head? Nothing. You know what I learned from people actually paying $3.99? Everything. When someone pays for something, even a small amount, you learn what they value.

### 2. Go Where the Users Are, Don't Make Them Come to You

I wasted months trying to build audiences on my own domain. Nobody wakes up thinking "I should visit maxlibin.com." They wake up thinking "I need to solve problem X."

So now I build distribution into the product. Tulsk integrates with Slack, Telegram, and WhatsApp — because that's where people already work. HomeworkAI lives on parents' phones, not on a web dashboard they'll forget to check.

### 3. Build in Public, But With Purpose

Building in public is the standard advice for solo founders in 2026. I do it — I post updates, share metrics, show screenshots. But what I've learned is that the *content* of the public build matters more than the *fact* of it.

The posts that get attention aren't "I shipped another app today." They're "I made $47 this month from my latest app — here's exactly how." Or "Here's the exact prompt I used to build this feature in 20 minutes." Specificity wins. Real numbers win. Vulnerability wins — the posts about my apps that flopped get more engagement than the ones that worked.

### 4. Price for the Person, Not for the Category

I mentioned Tulsk's original pricing. For Interior AI, I tried something different: $2.99/week. That's a coffee. People don't deliberate over a coffee purchase. Subscription conversions went up 3x compared to a $9.99/month tier.

Does $2.99/week sound indie? Yes. Does it work? Also yes. I can always raise prices later once people are hooked on the value.

## The Uncomfortable Truth

As I write this, I'm 7 apps away from completing the Vibe Code to Glory challenge. I know I'll ship all 24. The code will get written, the deployments will go through, and the checkmarks will fill up.

But I also know that most of those 24 apps won't make meaningful revenue.

That's not failure. That's reality for anyone building in 2026. The cost of building has dropped so low that the signal is no longer "can you build it?" — the signal is "can you get anyone to care?"

For the remaining 7 apps, I'm not optimizing for lines of code shipped or features built. I'm optimizing for one thing: **can I get 10 people to pay for this before I build the next feature?**

If the answer is no, I pivot or kill it. That's the solo founder's discipline that AI hasn't automated — and probably never will.
